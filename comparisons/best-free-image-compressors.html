Best Free Image Compressors: 2026 Comparison

Large images slow down websites, bloat mobile apps, and eat storage on every platform they touch. A good image compressor shrinks file size without visible quality loss, but the tools that do this vary widely in format support, compression ratio, and privacy. We compared six popular image compression services to see which ones deliver the best results and which ones protect your data while doing it.

UseEasyTool Image Compressor

UseEasyTool's image compressor runs entirely in your browser using WebAssembly and the Canvas API. When you drop an image into the tool, the compression happens locally: the file is never uploaded to a server, which means you can compress confidential screenshots, client photography, or internal assets without any data leaving your device. This is especially important for photographers, designers, and businesses handling proprietary imagery.

The tool supports JPEG, PNG, WebP, and GIF, covering the four formats that account for the vast majority of images on the web. A quality slider lets you control the trade-off between file size and visual fidelity, and a live before-and-after preview shows exactly how much degradation the selected compression level introduces. For PNG files specifically, the compressor applies lossless palette reduction that can cut file size by 30 to 70 percent without altering a single pixel.

Batch processing is built in. You can drag multiple files at once, and the tool processes them sequentially, showing the original and compressed size for each file along with the total savings. There is no file count limit and no per-file size cap, because the processing happens on your machine using your own CPU rather than a shared server. This makes the tool suitable for compressing large batches of high-resolution photos that would exceed the daily limits of server-side alternatives.

TinyPNG Review

TinyPNG is the most well-known name in online image compression, and it earned that reputation with smart PNG palette reduction that consistently shrinks files by 50 to 80 percent with no visible quality loss. The JPEG compression is also strong, using quantization techniques that preserve detail better than a naive quality reduction. The interface is clean, and the batch upload limit of 20 files is enough for most casual users.

The main limitation is that TinyPNG processes images server-side. When you upload a file, it travels to TinyPNG's servers, gets compressed there, and is sent back. The company states that uploaded files are deleted after a short period, but for users with strict data policies, any server-side upload is a non-starter. The free web interface also limits files to 5 MB each, and the API requires a paid plan beyond 500 compressions per month. For quick, non-sensitive tasks, TinyPNG is excellent. For privacy-sensitive work, it requires careful consideration.

Squoosh Review

Squoosh, developed by the Google Chrome team, is a technically impressive tool that performs compression entirely client-side using WebAssembly codecs. It supports JPEG, PNG, WebP, AVIF, and other modern formats, and its split-screen comparison view is one of the best in any tool, letting you pan and zoom to inspect compression artifacts at a pixel level. The codec options are extensive, giving you fine-grained control over quality, color depth, and dithering.

The trade-off is that Squoosh is built for single-image precision work, not batch processing. There is no way to compress multiple files at once, which makes it impractical when you need to optimize a folder of product photos or a set of blog images. The interface, while powerful, can also feel overwhelming for users who just want a simple compress-and-download workflow. Squoosh is the right tool when you need to fine-tune a single hero image to perfection, but it is not a batch compressor.

Compressor.io Review

Compressor.io offers a clean interface and supports JPEG, PNG, WebP, GIF, and SVG, which is a broader format range than most competitors. The tool provides two compression modes: lossless, which preserves pixel-for-pixel accuracy while reducing metadata and optimizing encoding, and lossy, which trades some quality for smaller file sizes. The results are competitive, particularly for JPEG, where Compressor.io routinely achieves 60 to 80 percent reduction.

Like TinyPNG, Compressor.io processes images server-side, which raises the same privacy considerations. The free tier limits files to 10 MB, and batch processing is not available on the free plan, requiring a paid subscription. The tool also lacks a quality slider on the free tier, so you cannot fine-tune the compression level; you are limited to the preset lossless or lossy modes. For users who want server-side convenience and do not mind the upload, Compressor.io is a solid option. For privacy-conscious users, the server-side model is a drawback.

Why Browser-Based Compression Wins

The fundamental question with any online image tool is where the processing happens. Server-side compressors upload your image to a remote server, compress it there, and send the result back. Client-side compressors do everything in your browser using WebAssembly, Canvas, and Web Workers, meaning the image never leaves your device. This distinction matters more than most users realize.

Privacy is the most obvious advantage. Photographers compress client images, designers compress proprietary mockups, and businesses compress product photography that has not yet been released. When you upload these files to a server-side tool, you are transferring them to a third party that may store, process, or retain them. Even if the service promises deletion, you are trusting their infrastructure, their logging configuration, and their security posture. A single misconfigured server can expose thousands of uploaded images. Client-side processing removes this risk entirely because the image data stays in your browser's memory and is discarded when you close the tab.

Performance and cost are the other advantages. Server-side tools impose file size limits and daily quotas because compression consumes server CPU, and those resources cost money. Client-side tools use your own CPU, so there is no per-file fee and no artificial limit. This means you can compress a folder of 50 high-resolution photos without hitting a paywall or waiting in a queue. For anyone who compresses images regularly, the browser-based approach is faster, more private, and free of usage caps.

How We Tested

We tested each tool with a set of 12 images: four JPEG photos (ranging from 2 MB to 8 MB), four PNG screenshots with text and UI elements, two WebP images, and two animated GIFs. For each tool, we recorded the compression ratio, visual quality via side-by-side comparison, processing time, and whether the tool processed the image client-side or server-side using browser developer tools. We also checked format support, batch capabilities, and file size limits. Where tools offered both lossless and lossy modes, we tested both and reported the better result for each image type.

FAQ

Which image compressor gives the best quality?

Quality depends on the format and the compression settings rather than the tool itself. TinyPNG and Compressor.io produce excellent results for server-side tools, while Squoosh offers the most fine-grained control for single images. UseEasyTool and Squoosh both process client-side, so they match server-side quality without uploading your files. The best approach is to test a few tools with your specific images and compare the results visually.

Do these tools upload my images?

It depends on the tool. UseEasyTool and Squoosh process images entirely in your browser, so nothing is uploaded. TinyPNG, Compressor.io, Optimizilla, and ILoveIMG all upload images to their servers for processing. If your images are confidential, sensitive, or proprietary, a client-side tool is the safer choice. Always check the tool's privacy policy before uploading sensitive content.

What's the maximum file size?

UseEasyTool has no file size limit because processing happens on your device. Server-side tools impose limits: TinyPNG caps files at 5 MB on the free tier, Compressor.io at 10 MB, and ILoveIMG varies by plan. These limits exist because server-side compression consumes shared resources. If you regularly work with large files, a client-side tool avoids this constraint entirely.

Should I use WebP instead of JPEG?

In most cases, yes. WebP produces files 25 to 35 percent smaller than JPEG at equivalent visual quality, and all major browsers have supported it since 2020. The exception is if you need maximum compatibility with very old systems or email clients that do not render WebP. For websites, WebP is the recommended format today, and several tools in this comparison can convert to WebP during compression.

Can I compress images in bulk?

UseEasyTool, TinyPNG, Optimizilla, and ILoveIMG support batch processing. Squoosh and Compressor.io do not offer batch on their free tiers. UseEasyTool has no batch file limit since processing is client-side, while server-side tools cap batch uploads (TinyPNG at 20 files, others vary by plan). If you regularly compress dozens of images, look for a tool with robust batch support and no count limits.
